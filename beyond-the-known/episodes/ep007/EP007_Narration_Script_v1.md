## Chapter 1 — A Hole in the Ground

February 1981. In the Pennsylvania coal town of Centralia, the ground suddenly gave way beneath a twelve-year-old boy. Todd Domboski caught hold of tree roots as a deep, steaming sinkhole opened below him. His cousin helped pull him to safety.

The ground was releasing dangerous fumes. This was not a natural cave collapse or a hidden volcano. For almost nineteen years, a fire had been moving through the coal underground.

Centralia's roads, houses and church had been built over old mine workings. Every street looked ordinary until the danger reached the surface.

How does a town begin to burn from the inside out without anyone seeing the flames?

## Chapter 2 — The Fire That Disappeared

Centralia was built around anthracite coal. Its mines supplied fuel and jobs, and empty underground tunnels remained when mining declined.

In May 1962, a fire broke out in an old open mining pit used for rubbish near the edge of town. Exactly how it was started has been debated. But what happened afterward is much clearer.

The flames reached exposed coal and moved beneath the surface, following seams and old mine passages. Firefighters could knock down visible flames and pour water into the area. That did not mean the underground fire was out.

Down below, fuel remained. Air could reach it through connected workings and fractures. The fire had found a way to travel without needing a visible blaze overhead.

## Chapter 3 — An Invisible Neighbour

Years passed. The fire kept spreading through parts of the coalfield beneath and around Centralia.

The danger was not always a wall of flames. It was hot ground, unstable soil, vents releasing smoke and steam, and gases that could enter enclosed buildings.

Carbon monoxide was especially frightening because it cannot be seen or smelled. Officials installed monitoring equipment, drilled test holes and tried different ways of controlling the fire.

But the old mining network made the problem exceptionally difficult. An attempt to seal one area could still leave hot coal and passages elsewhere. Sections of ground could weaken, and cracks could appear without much warning.

By the end of the nineteen seventies, Centralia's invisible problem was becoming impossible to ignore.

## Chapter 4 — The Price of Leaving

The boy's near disaster in 1981 brought national attention to a crisis already building for years.

State and federal agencies investigated how far the fire had spread. A government study in the early nineteen eighties estimated that extinguishing it could cost hundreds of millions of dollars.

The solution increasingly became relocation rather than putting out every underground flame.

In 1984, Congress funded a programme to buy affected properties and help families leave. Hundreds of residences and businesses were eventually acquired. Homes came down. Streets that had been full of neighbours grew quieter.

Not everyone wanted to go. Some people challenged the relocation programme and fought for the right to remain in the place they called home.

In the nineteen nineties, part of Route Sixty-One closed because of fire-related damage and subsidence.

## Chapter 5 — The Town Above the Fire

Today, much of the old town has disappeared. Grass and trees cover places where homes and shops once stood. The abandoned mine fire is still monitored by Pennsylvania authorities.

You might imagine an endless river of lava under every road. That is not what a coal-seam fire looks like. Its hot zones shift as underground fuel and air pathways change.

The continuing hazards include noxious gases, weakened ground and sudden subsidence. This is not somewhere to seek out steaming cracks for a photograph.

Centralia's tragedy began with a resource the town had depended upon. Coal built its economy, and later the abandoned mine workings carried a fire that helped dismantle the community.

More than six decades after the first blaze, no one can say exactly when the final underground embers will go dark.
