## Chapter 1 — The Signal

In August 1977, a radio telescope in Ohio picked up something that should have been easy to explain.

A narrow stream of radio energy grew stronger, reached a peak, and faded into silence.

The telescope had been surveying the sky. No one heard a voice. No message appeared on a screen. Just six characters, printed on paper: 6EQUJ5.

When volunteer astronomer Jerry Ehman later saw the readout, he circled the sequence and wrote one word in red ink.

Wow!

The signal had lasted for roughly seventy-two seconds in the telescope's view. It seemed to come from the direction of Sagittarius.

And in almost fifty years of searching, no one has confirmed seeing that same signal again.

One extraordinary observation.

One unanswered question.

What, exactly, did the telescope detect?

## Chapter 2 — Six Characters, Not a Message

The famous sequence looks like a secret code. But six E Q U J five is not an alien sentence.

Each character represented the strength of the signal in one small time interval. The reading rose steadily, peaked at U, then dropped again.

At its strongest, it was roughly thirty times the background level.

That shape matters. Big Ear, as the Ohio State telescope was called, was essentially fixed in place. Earth's rotation swept different parts of the sky through its narrow field of view.

A distant radio source drifting across the beam could produce just such a rise and fall.

And the frequency was intriguing too: close to fourteen hundred and twenty megahertz, near the natural radio emission of hydrogen.

Hydrogen is everywhere in the universe. For decades, scientists have considered this part of the spectrum an interesting place to search for unusual radio signals.

But an interesting frequency is not proof of a message.

And seventy-two seconds is the observation window, not a recording of someone speaking for seventy-two seconds.

## Chapter 3 — The Part That Should Have Happened Twice

There was another complication.

Big Ear used two receiving beams. As the sky drifted overhead, a stable signal from one position could be expected to pass through both, separated by a few minutes.

But the Wow! Signal appeared in only one.

Why?

Perhaps the source switched off. Perhaps the signal changed. Perhaps an earthly interference event imitated something cosmic.

Or perhaps this was a rare natural event that the instruments caught only once.

Astronomers searched again. Other observatories scanned the region, including modern radio arrays. No confirmed repetition appeared.

Without a second detection, scientists couldn't follow a changing signal back to a precise source.

And that is why the mystery survives.

It is tempting to fill the silence with a story about an alien civilisation.

The data cannot support that conclusion.

A signal can be real, unusual, and unexplained without being evidence of extraterrestrial life.

## Chapter 4 — A Natural Explanation?

For years, proposed explanations came and went.

Then researchers started looking again at archival radio observations, including cold clouds of hydrogen in space.

Some clouds can produce narrow radio emissions, and under unusual conditions a powerful transient event might brighten them dramatically.

Research published in twenty twenty-five re-examined old Ohio telescope data and refined the Wow! Signal's possible position and frequency. The authors argued that a natural astrophysical origin could fit the evidence better than ordinary radio interference.

That does not mean the mystery has been solved.

There is no identified source that everyone agrees caused the nineteen seventy-seven signal. The hydrogen-cloud idea is a hypothesis being tested, not a verdict.

But it changes the story.

Perhaps the strangest part was never that the signal looked artificial.

Perhaps it was that the universe can produce brief radio events we still struggle to recognise.

## Chapter 5 — What Would Finally Answer It?

Imagine hearing a single note from a song, then never hearing it again.

You could analyse the pitch. You might guess where it came from. But could you identify the musician?

That's the Wow! Signal.

To explain it, astronomers need a repetition, a matching physical source, or the same event captured by multiple telescopes.

Today's instruments are far more sensitive than Big Ear, but the essential evidence is still missing.

There is no confirmed alien message. No decoded language. Only a real, unexplained radio observation.

For seventy-two seconds, an unusual signal crossed one telescope's view. Then it disappeared.

The mystery isn't what it was trying to say.

It's what made it happen.

## Science and image guardrails
- Event recorded August 15 1977 by Ohio State's Big Ear fixed radio telescope; Ehman wrote Wow! on a printed data readout later, not a giant live computer display. 6EQUJ5 represents successive signal strength readings, NOT text or an alien sentence; do not dub it as actual received sound or alien language.
- Duration ~72 seconds is how long a sky position transited telescope reception; unknown intrinsic event start or end.
- Approximately 1420 MHz hydrogen line region; stronger narrowband signal, peaking roughly 30 times background. The exact emission point and physical nature remain uncertain. The two adjacent telescope receiving beams recorded signal in only one.
- No confirmed repetition despite follow-up searches. The frequency/shape are compelling but cannot establish extraterrestrial technology.
- 2025 archival reanalysis by Méndez et al. revised radio source constraints and favoured a potentially natural astrophysical hydrogen-cloud phenomenon. Hypothesis remains unconfirmed, not definitively solved.
- Cinematic period 1970s observational equipment, authentic dot matrix/line printer paper, handwritten red 'Wow!' recreated as original art (do not copy archive image 1:1); modern observatories distinct; dark star fields, spectrum visualisations, astrophysical gas clouds and signal physics, no fantasy UFO craft or alien creature shots.
