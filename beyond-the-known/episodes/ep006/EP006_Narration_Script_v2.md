## Chapter 1 — The Head That Moved

June twelfth, nineteen sixty-two. A prison guard checked the cells on Alcatraz Island. Three prisoners appeared to be sleeping. Then a guard touched one man's head. It rolled from the pillow. The nose broke. The face was a dummy made from plaster, paint and real hair. The real prisoner was gone.

So were two others.

Frank Morris, and brothers John and Clarence Anglin, had vanished from America's most notorious island prison overnight. The authorities locked down Alcatraz and searched the buildings. But the three men were already missing, along with an extraordinary piece of homemade equipment.

For decades, people have asked whether they survived. To understand the mystery, we have to look at what happened before the guards discovered those empty beds.

## Chapter 2 — The Island Nobody Escaped

Alcatraz stood alone in San Francisco Bay. Its reputation rested on strict routines, watchful guards and the cold, fast-moving water separating the island from shore.

The three escapees were no strangers to prison. Frank Morris had a history of escaping custody. The Anglin brothers had known him before arriving at Alcatraz. Another prisoner, Allen West, joined their plan.

The men occupied neighbouring cells. Behind their cell walls lay a service area, largely outside the guards' view. Over months, the prisoners secretly gained access to that space and made their way to an unused area above the cell block.

There, they prepared for a journey that would have seemed absurd to the guards below: crossing open water at night on a makeshift raft. At the same time, they made the fake sleeping heads that would buy them precious hours.

Everything depended on whether their preparations could survive one particular night.

## Chapter 3 — The Night of the Escape

On June eleventh, the plan went into motion. Allen West couldn't leave his cell as intended. He remained behind.

Morris and the two Anglin brothers disappeared into the prison's concealed spaces. They reached the roof, descended to the outside grounds, and moved toward the island's northeast shore. Inside their beds lay the three dummy heads. As guards performed their rounds, nothing looked obviously wrong.

The men had transformed more than fifty raincoats into a raft and makeshift life preservers. Now, in darkness, they launched that equipment into San Francisco Bay.

Getting out of the prison was only half an escape. The bay was icy, and powerful tides could sweep their raft off course. The island's lights were fading behind them, but dry land was still far away.

From that moment, there are no confirmed eyewitness accounts of what happened to all three men.

## Chapter 4 — What the Water Left Behind

When authorities began searching, the bay offered fragments rather than answers.

A packet of personal effects linked to the men was recovered. Investigators later found pieces associated with their equipment, including a homemade life preserver washed ashore.

Allen West told investigators that the escape plan involved reaching Angel Island and then continuing toward Marin County. If the men reached land, they would still need a way to disappear.

But the FBI found no confirmed evidence of the escapees stealing a vehicle or clothing, or of anyone reliably seeing the trio alive. Searchers never recovered three bodies, either.

The FBI's investigators concluded the men probably failed to survive the crossing. Yet that conclusion was not physical proof of their deaths. The missing raft and missing men created a story that would refuse to close.

## Chapter 5 — The Unsolved Ending

People have spent decades studying the tides, proposing alternative routes and imagining where the escapees might have gone. Some claim the men reached land and lived under new identities. Others think the sea claimed them before sunrise.

Neither story has been conclusively proved.

After seventeen years of following leads, the FBI closed its own investigation on December thirty-first, nineteen seventy-nine. The United States Marshals Service retained responsibility for the case.

What is certain is remarkable enough. Three prisoners fooled a maximum-security institution with fake heads, vanished through its buildings, and launched an improvised raft into one of America's most difficult stretches of water.

Maybe they made it ashore. Maybe they didn't.

Alcatraz was supposed to be impossible to escape. The men proved that its walls could be beaten.

Whether they escaped the bay is the mystery no one can settle.
